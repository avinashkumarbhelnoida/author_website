How Writing Found Me

Avinash Kumar

Writing was never on my list. Reading was.

I was never one of those people who grew up knowing that one day they would become a writer. I simply had an enduring curiosity about things I did not know.

A new subject would appear, and my first instinct was not to have an opinion about it.

It was to read.

Because how can you have a meaningful thought about something you have never really explored?

So I read.

And slowly, something began to change.

Reading did more than give me information. It gave me perspective.

And perspective has a curious way of changing perception.

The more I read, the more I began to notice things I had previously overlooked. Ordinary behaviour started carrying extraordinary questions. Conversations had layers. Relationships had patterns. People were rarely as simple as they appeared. The past seemed to leave fingerprints on the present. A workplace was no longer merely a place where people worked. Love was no longer merely an emotion. Mythology was no longer merely a collection of ancient stories. Even the familiar workings of the mind began to look strangely unfamiliar.

I began to pause.

To question.

To connect.

And then I started making notes.

Not because I intended to write a book.

I simply did not want to lose a thought.

A line I had read.

An idea that stayed with me.

A psychological nuance.

A question that suddenly appeared.

A connection between two seemingly unrelated things.

A takeaway that made me look at an old experience differently.

I began rewriting some of those thoughts in my own words.

Then came more notes.

And more questions.

And then, inevitably, more subjects.

The mind. Memory. Behaviour. Relationships. Love. Men and women. Childhood. The past. Workplaces. Identity. Self-help. Mythology. Reality. The subconscious. The things we see—and the things that might exist beyond what we see.

Every answer seemed to open another door.

And behind that door was another question.

Somewhere along that journey, something unexpected happened.

Reading began to turn into writing.

I wasn't writing because I had decided I had something important to say.

I was writing because I had discovered something I could not stop thinking about.

And perhaps that is how most meaningful writing begins—not with the ambition to become a writer, but with the inability to let an idea go.

Today, I write across different subjects and genres, but the impulse remains the same.

Curiosity.

The belief that there is always something more to understand.

Something beneath the obvious.

Something behind the behaviour.

Something hidden inside the relationship.

Something carried from the past.

Something happening inside the mind.

Something waiting to be questioned.

I don't think a writer necessarily needs to know everything.

Perhaps the writer simply needs to be willing to say:

"I don't know enough about this. Let me find out."

That sentence has taken me a surprisingly long way.

From reading to observing.

From observing to questioning.

From questioning to making notes.

From notes to ideas.

And from ideas... to books.

So, if you ask me when I started writing, I wouldn't know exactly what date to give you.

Perhaps I started writing the day I stopped reading merely to know—and started reading to understand.

Because somewhere between those two things, I found my voice.

Writing was never on my list.

But reading was.

And perhaps writing was simply where reading eventually took me.
